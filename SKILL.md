# Harada Goal Decomposition

Help the user turn a meaningful goal into a system of action: goal and purpose → eight supporting areas → actions → a near-term plan, routines, and regular review. The matrix is a map, not a guarantee of success or a single-day to-do list. Communicate in the user's language.

## Choose a mode

- **New goal:** follow the steps below. Aim for a complete Open Window 64 when the user requests a full breakdown. If they ask for only part of the method or a short plan, honor that scope.
- **Continue or review:** first find the previously agreed goal, matrix, and latest results in the conversation or user-provided materials. Do not start over. Compare intentions with actual results, identify obstacles, and propose changes while preserving what works. If the history is unavailable, say so and ask for the minimum missing context.

## 1. Define the goal and starting conditions

Describe one desired outcome: what will change, by when, and which observable criterion will indicate success. Distinguish outcomes affected by external circumstances from actions under the user's control. Account for the starting point, resources, available time, constraints, dependencies, and previous experience. If the goal is too broad, propose a first stage with a measurable outcome that connects to the larger goal.

Explore meaning through four perspectives: tangible and intangible benefits for the user, and tangible and intangible benefits for others. Do not invent an impact on others or impose a mission; mark unclear perspectives as such.

Identify strengths and one or two skills or conditions especially important to the goal. This is a practical self-assessment, not a diagnosis or the complete official 33-item assessment.

Ask only for consequential information that cannot reasonably be inferred, grouping questions. If the user wants a draft immediately, proceed with clearly labeled assumptions and do not present them as facts. When an answer is essential, prepare the independent parts and mark dependent decisions as open.

## 2. Build the Open Window 64

Choose eight distinct areas that together support the goal. Connect each to a specific outcome or condition for success; include skills, support, resources, and recovery when relevant. Avoid overlapping or generic areas unrelated to the goal.

Place the goal in the center of a 3 × 3 grid and the eight areas around it. Repeat each area in the center of its own outer 3 × 3 grid; the eight surrounding cells hold its actions or verifiable steps. There are 81 cells: one goal, eight areas in the central grid, eight repeated area labels in the outer grid centers, and 64 outer entries (8 × 8).

Present the matrix so the connections stay clear: a central 3 × 3 table followed by eight numbered lists of eight entries, or an equivalent 9 × 9 grid. The outer block headings must match the central areas. Phrase actions as verbs and specify an outcome, amount, or frequency where useful. Distinguish one-off steps, recurring practices, and hypothesis checks; do not present habits as one-off tasks.

If the available information is insufficient for 64 useful entries, show the sound actions already available and mark remaining cells as open questions or hypotheses. Do not fill the grid with duplication or invented facts. Complete it when sufficient information is available. Do not imply that all 64 entries must be executed.

## 3. Turn the map into an executable plan

- Identify stages, dependencies, and checkpoints. Match dates and workload to the user's actual capacity; label assumptions when capacity is unknown.
- Select a small set of actions for the next 7–14 days. For each, state a concrete result, the next physical action, and a deadline or starting condition. Identify a first step feasible within 24 hours when realistic.
- From recurring actions, suggest one to three manageable routines with frequency, context, and a minimum version for a difficult day. Explain how to track them and account for planned rest.
- Identify at least one likely obstacle and an “if …, then …” response. Separately state what help or decisions are needed from other people.
- Offer a short weekly review: what was done, what result emerged, what got in the way, what to change, and the next step. A review is a suggested user practice; do not create reminders or promise automatic follow-up without a corresponding request and available tool.

## 4. Check that the plan is usable

Ensure each active action is clear without further guesswork, the first step is accessible, the workload fits known constraints, action metrics differ from the outcome, and assumptions and external dependencies are visible. The matrix and plan can change as new information arrives. When relevant, note that financial, medical, legal, or other sensitive goals require reliable current information and appropriate professional help.

Do not claim that the method scientifically guarantees results or that self-assessment is diagnostic. Planning alone does not authorize saving to memory, creating external tasks, or acting on the user's behalf; those actions require a corresponding request and appropriate permissions.
